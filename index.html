KINGSMEN ACADEMY // FIELD MANUAL

Theory

Reaction time isn't one skill — it's detection, decision, movement, and prediction, stacked together. A 170 ms player who predicts well will beat a 150 ms player who doesn't. Here's what actually moves the needle, and what doesn't.

A Four-Part Model
Detection — spotting the stimulus. Trained by Base Reflex, Peripheral Ping, Flash Reflex.
Decision — picking the right response among options. Trained by Choice Reaction, Go/No-Go, Parity Rush.
Movement — executing the click cleanly. Trained by Trigger Discipline, Flanker Task.
Prediction — recognizing a pattern so the decision is already made. Trained by Simon Sequence, Symbol 1-Back.

The first three are the actual processing chain, in order. Prediction isn't a fourth step in that chain — it's what lets you skip most of it, which is exactly why it's worth training separately. Shaving 10 ms off raw detection is genuinely hard. Shaving 30–80 ms off decision time through pattern recognition and experience is often realistic — and matters more in a real game than a faster raw reflex.

How Fast Can a Human Get?
LevelVisual reaction
Average adult245–280 ms
Above average220–245 ms
Competitive gamer180–220 ms
Excellent150–180 ms
Elite130–150 ms
Exceptional, consistently repeatableUnder 130 ms

These are click-measured numbers, which run slower than lab studies using a button box — the click itself costs you time. That's deliberate: it's the same way this app measures, so the bands above line up with the rank tiers you'll actually be given (Silver sits on the population average, Gold just above it).

Below ~120 ms as a sustained average is extraordinarily rare — you're pushing against nerve conduction and muscle activation limits, not just skill. Isolated sub-110 ms trials happen; holding that average over many trials essentially doesn't. Watch for anticipation: a predictable rhythm or a narrow random interval lets people unconsciously guess. A test measuring genuine reaction needs wide, unpredictable timing and should penalize early clicks.

Genetics — the ceiling

Sets your floor and ceiling more than anything else on this list. Essentially no room to change these directly — the rest of this page is about training around them.

  • Nerve conduction velocity
  • Myelination of neurons
  • Muscle fiber composition
  • Baseline dopamine levels
  • Visual processing speed & brain structure
  • Age — peaks in the early-to-mid 20s, but the drop-off is gentle: simple reaction barely slows until around 50, while choice reaction (picking between options) declines steadily right through adulthood
Sleep — probably the biggest lever

One bad night can cost you 20–50 ms. Nothing else on this page moves the number that much that fast.

  • 8–9 hours of quality sleep
  • Consistent sleep schedule
  • Eliminate accumulated sleep debt
  • Treat sleep apnea if present
  • Avoid alcohol before bed
  • Dim screens and lights before bed — overall brightness and what you're doing matter more than blue light specifically
  • Keep the room cool
Vision & monitor setup

Your eyes are the input device — everything downstream depends on what they hand off.

  • Correct prescription, worn consistently
  • Reduce dry eyes, blink normally
  • Good, consistent room lighting
  • Top of monitor around eye level
  • Center of screen directly ahead
  • About an arm's length away

Dominant eye positioning

Some FPS professionals sit slightly offset so their dominant eye is more centered on the screen — the idea being less binocular conflict and marginally lighter visual processing demand. It's a plausible mechanism, but the measured effect is small and the evidence is thin (see "Weak or unproven claims" below); worth trying if you're already tweaking your setup, not worth rearranging your desk for.

Lean peeking

Siege's Q/E lean exposes only a sliver of your model while keeping your own view mostly stable — that stability is exactly what keeps detection time low on a lean-peek trade instead of adding a second re-focus cost on top of the reaction itself.

Hardware & input

This is the one category that can cut effective reaction time without touching your actual reflexes — the frame the stimulus first appears on is a hard floor under everything else.

Refresh rateFrame interval
60 Hz16.7 ms
144 Hz6.9 ms
240 Hz4.2 ms
360 Hz2.8 ms
540 Hz1.85 ms

Read those as granularity, not as a straight saving. An enemy can appear at any moment within a frame, so on average you wait about half an interval for the screen to show it — going 60 Hz to 240 Hz buys you roughly 6 ms on average, not 12.5.

And when researchers held latency constant and varied only refresh rate, the refresh rate itself mattered surprisingly little above 60 Hz — most of what a high-Hz monitor gives you is the lower latency that comes packaged with it. Chasing end-to-end latency is the better spend; going from 240 Hz to 360 Hz on its own is near the bottom of this list.

  • Low-persistence display
  • Fast pixel response
  • Low input-lag monitor (check response time, not just Hz)
  • High polling-rate mouse & keyboard
  • Stable high FPS — dips hurt more than a lower average helps
  • NVIDIA Reflex / AMD Anti-Lag
  • Strip out unnecessary latency sources (wireless dongles, overlays, V-Sync where it adds lag)
Nutrition

Hydration

  • Even mild dehydration measurably slows reaction time
  • Worsens attention
  • Degrades motor control

Protein & carbohydrates

  • Protein supports neurotransmitter production, recovery, and general brain function
  • The brain runs on glucose — too little carb intake means slower thinking and reduced attention
  • Too much in one sitting causes post-meal sluggishness
  • Most esports athletes perform best after a moderate meal, not a heavy or empty one

Omega-3 & micronutrients

  • Omega-3 may support neuronal membrane function and cognitive performance — a long-term investment, not an instant fix
  • Deficiencies in iron, B12, folate, magnesium, zinc, or vitamin D all measurably hurt cognitive performance if you're actually low

Caffeine

  • One of the strongest legal reaction enhancers available — typically worth 5–20 ms
  • Best dose is roughly 2–3 mg per kg of bodyweight
  • Too much causes jitter, worse aim, and anxiety — costing back more than the caffeine gained

Creatine, nitrates, L-theanine

  • Creatine — small cognitive benefits, particularly under sleep deprivation
  • Beetroot nitrates — may improve cerebral blood flow, though evidence is mixed
  • L-theanine + caffeine — reasonably supported for smoother focus and less jitter than caffeine alone
Physical fitness

Elite gamers increasingly train physically — exercise improves blood flow, executive function, attention, and reaction speed directly, not just general health.

  • Cardio: running, cycling, swimming, rowing
  • Strength training — improves motor unit recruitment, movement speed, and fatigue resistance
  • Neck & postural strength — helps comfort and fatigue over long sessions; the head-stability research behind it comes from contact sports, not gaming
Motor learning & practice

Reaction isn't only seeing — it's moving. These are trainable motor skills, not fixed traits:

  • Mouse flicks
  • Click timing
  • Finger speed
  • Keyboard precision

Eventually the movement becomes automatic, freeing up conscious attention for the decision itself.

Training the brain

This is where the modules in this app come in — each one isolates a specific piece of the reaction chain instead of training the whole thing as one vague blur.

  • Simple reaction — a stimulus appears, you respond (a peeking enemy, a red gadget light)
  • Choice reaction — different stimuli map to different responses (operator/gadget ID, which angle to swing)
  • Go / No-Go — respond to one signal, withhold on another (the exact skill behind not shooting a teammate through a smoke or a headglitch)
  • Multiple object tracking & peripheral vision — tracking a drone read, a cam, and a doorway at once without losing any of them
  • Pattern recognition — turn "see, think, decide" into "see, already know" (recognizing a default plant or a common roam spot instantly)

Prediction training

The biggest gap between professionals and average players. Pros rarely react to a raw stimulus — they predict it (a rotate about to open, a common re-peek timing), so the reaction effectively starts before the event happens.

What this kind of training can and can't do

Worth being straight about: reaction training transfers narrowly. You mostly get better at the specific task you trained, and the gains shrink the further the drill sits from the real thing. That's the actual reason the generic "brain training" apps listed further down don't work — not that training is useless, but that training something unrelated to what you do buys you nothing at the thing you care about.

It's also the standard these modules are built to: Siege-shaped cues, go/no-go on teammates, vertical directional bias, wide unpredictable timing that punishes guessing. The honest expectation is sharper individual components and a clear, tracked picture of your own numbers and weak spots — not a global reaction upgrade that follows you into every game you play.

Stress, arousal & warm-up

High anxiety

  • Slower decisions
  • Tunnel vision
  • Muscle tension

What helps

  • Breathing exercises
  • Experience & confidence
  • Repeated exposure

The goal isn't to be as calm as possible — there's a sweet spot between too relaxed (slow) and too stressed (inaccurate) where reactions are both fast and consistent. That's the Yerkes–Dodson relationship, if you want the term for it.

This is the exact gap between a player who plays clean in ranked or scrims and one who tunnel-visions and whiffs the read in a 1v1 for match point — same mechanical skill, different arousal level.

The stakes spike pushes them past the peak of the curve: heart rate climbs, attention narrows onto whatever feels most threatening instead of what's actually relevant, and reflexes that were automatic a minute earlier start needing conscious effort, which is both slower and more error-prone. It's also why scrims don't fully prepare you for that moment either; scrim pressure is real, but it rarely matches the arousal spike of an elimination round or a LAN crowd, so the adaptation has to come from deliberately practicing under comparably high-stakes conditions, not just mechanically identical ones.

Warm-up

  • Most people react measurably faster after 10–20 minutes of warm-up
  • Raises nerve conduction speed
  • Raises muscle temperature
  • Raises general alertness
Game knowledge & deliberate practice

Arguably the single biggest "reaction speed" increase available — and it isn't physiological at all. In Siege specifically, knowing these reduces decision time enormously:

  • Headglitches, common angles, and where hard breach opens a new one mid-round
  • Reinforcement, rotate, and drone-scan timings for the site being played
  • Operator tendencies — which roamers hold which spots, who plays aggro vs. anchors
  • Audio cues — footsteps through soft walls, drone/cam beeps, gadget deploy sounds (Kaid claw, Jäger ADS, Bandit battery hum), breach charges
  • Utility usage — knowing what a sound or a destroyed gadget tells you about intent

A player with a 170 ms simple reaction who's acting on expectation will often beat a 150 ms player reacting to genuine surprise — this is most of what separates a ranked grinder from a pro in Siege specifically, since so much of the game is pre-fight information, not raw aim.

Measure the right things

  • Average reaction time
  • Consistency (standard deviation)
  • Directional bias — left, right, up, down (Siege plays vertically: hatches, rappel, destruction)
  • Friend/foe mix-ups under pressure — hesitation or misfires on teammates
  • Fatigue over a session
  • Improvement curve over time

Consistency usually matters more than any one fast trial — which is why this app tracks accuracy and full-run history alongside best times, not just personal bests in isolation.

Environment & avoiding fatigue
  • Comfortable temperature, good posture, quiet room
  • Comfortable chair, consistent setup
  • Mouse sensitivity that matches your grip and monitor size

Reaction time steadily worsens with long sessions, sleep deprivation, hunger, and dehydration. Many professional teams schedule breaks roughly every hour specifically to protect reaction performance, not just to rest.

Weak or unproven claims

Commonly repeated, but the evidence is thin or the effect is tiny. Worth knowing about, not worth building a routine around.

  • Sitting with the dominant eye more centered on the monitor — plausible mechanism, very small measured effect
  • Vision-training glasses
  • Blue-light gaming glasses — unless they're reducing eye strain over long sessions, which is a separate benefit
  • General "brain training" apps unrelated to the actual task
  • Nootropics beyond caffeine — most have little high-quality supporting evidence
  • Ginkgo biloba, Rhodiola rosea
  • Lion's Mane — interesting research direction, no strong evidence yet for faster gaming reactions
  • Binaural beats
  • Colored room lighting
